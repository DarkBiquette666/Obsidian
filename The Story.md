Detective Davar was a man carved from the grit of the city streets, his soul weathered like old leather from too many years spent in the shadows of corruption and violence. The city had taken its toll on him, leaving behind a shell of the idealistic young officer he once was. Now, he sat in his dimly lit office, the stench of stale coffee and cigarette smoke clinging to the air like the memories he couldn’t shake. His once sharp suit was now a wrinkled mess, the collar stained with sweat from countless sleepless nights.

Davar had seen it all. Murders, robberies, betrayals—nothing surprised him anymore. The city was a beast, devouring its own, and he had long ago accepted that he couldn’t save it. Hell, most days he couldn’t even save himself. His marriage was a casualty of the job, his kids estranged, and the only family he had left was a bottle of whiskey tucked away in his desk drawer.

It was late when the call came in—another body found in the alleys of Old Town. A young woman, beaten and strangled. Nothing special, just another statistic in a city that was drowning in its own filth. But something about this one didn’t sit right with Davar. Maybe it was the way the body had been left, posed almost like a sick offering. Or maybe it was the feeling gnawing at the back of his mind, a whisper that this was only the beginning.

He dragged his weary body to the scene, the rain pouring down in sheets, washing the blood from the cracked pavement. The rookie cops on duty flinched when they saw him, not out of fear, but pity. They knew the stories, knew how the years had hollowed him out, leaving only a bitter husk. But Davar didn’t care what they thought. He’d outlasted better men than them.

As he knelt beside the body, something flickered in his chest—a faint, forgotten ember of the fire he used to have. This one was different. This one was personal. And for the first time in years, Davar felt something other than apathy. He didn’t know if it was rage, sorrow, or desperation. But whatever it was, it pulled him back in.

The commissioner stood, his eyes hard, and lit another cigarette. He’d seen too much to expect justice. But revenge? That was something he could work with.

And this city still owed him.

The rain beat down on the streets, thick like a curtain drawn over the city’s filth. Commissioner Davar’s office reeked of cheap bourbon and the decay of old memories. His eyes, bloodshot and heavy, scanned the room, taking in the things that still mattered. Not much. A few photos collecting dust on his desk, a gun in the top drawer, and the bitter taste of regret on his tongue.

He stared into the darkness, the dim flicker of a streetlamp cutting through the blinds, casting a sickly glow on the bottle in front of him. His hand trembled as he poured another glass, the amber liquid sloshing like some pathetic elixir meant to keep his demons at bay. He drank deep, feeling the burn in his throat, but it wasn’t enough. It never was.

The call had come an hour ago. Another dead girl. Young, like they always were. But this one...this one felt like a goddamn ghost. It reminded him of things he’d spent years burying, deep beneath layers of booze, cigarettes, and lies. He wasn’t ready to dig it up, not yet. Not alone.

As he reached for his coat, his hand grazed the worn cushion on the chair in the corner. Anthony’s cushion. The faded wine stain still visible in the dim light—a mark from a night years ago, when Anthony had been alive, laughing, spilling that goddamn bottle everywhere. They’d sat there, partners in more than just the badge, their bodies slumped in the cushions, pretending they could save each other from the city’s slow rot.

Davar clenched his jaw. Anthony. Always so fucking hopeful, until hope wasn’t enough. Until that hope had gotten him killed. The cushion was the only thing left of him now, that and the guilt that gnawed at Davar’s insides like a rabid dog. He’d promised Anthony they’d make it through, but promises didn’t mean shit in a world like this.

The body in Old Town wasn’t just another case. It had Anthony written all over it. The way the girl’s legs were crossed at the ankles, her arms draped neatly by her sides—posed, like someone was recreating a memory. Someone who knew exactly how to cut into Davar’s rotting soul.

He slammed the glass down hard enough to crack it, but didn’t flinch. Blood trickled from his hand, mixing with the whiskey on the table. His mind flashed back to the funeral, Anthony’s wife, pale and broken, clutching a bottle of wine as if it could fill the hole Davar had left in their lives. He’d never gone to her after that. Never explained the blood on his hands. He wasn’t sure she’d survive the truth.

Now, years later, it was all coming back, crawling out of the gutter like some diseased thing. Whoever had killed the girl had a message for him, and it reeked of Anthony. The taste of stale cigarettes and bitter memories clung to Davar’s tongue as he grabbed his coat and made his way out into the rain-soaked streets.

Old Town was where it always started, and where it would end. A labyrinth of abandoned buildings, shattered glass, and the stench of piss soaked into the concrete. He found the alley, lit only by the dull flicker of a dying neon sign. The body was still there, untouched, waiting for him. Her eyes were open, staring at nothing, just like Anthony’s had been when they’d pulled him from the wreckage. The girl wasn’t the real victim here.

Davar’s cigarette burned low, the smoke curling up into the night like a prayer to a god long dead. His heart was numb, but his mind was awake, sharper than it had been in years. The city had taken Anthony from him, but it hadn’t killed the beast inside. That beast was awake now, ready to tear through flesh, ready to make someone bleed.

The rain kept falling, washing away the blood, the tears, the dirt. But the stain on Anthony’s cushion? That would never fade.

Davar lit another cigarette as the rain soaked him through. The alley was drenched in shadow and neon, every drop of water reflecting the city's endless misery. He crouched by the body, already cold and stiff, her pale skin catching the flickering light. The way she was posed, legs crossed, arms limp—it was a sick echo of something that gnawed at his insides. Whoever did this wasn’t in a rush. They had taken their time.

The girl's dress was torn, the fabric stained with blood and something else, something that made Davar’s stomach churn in disgust. He'd seen bodies broken in every way possible, but this—this was different. It wasn’t just murder. It was defilement. The skin beneath her thighs glistened under the rain, smeared with semen, left there like a calling card. Whoever had done this wanted him to see. Wanted him to feel it.

His mind flashed to Anthony, slumped against that cushion, still alive then, blood pooling in his mouth, coughing up promises he couldn’t keep. “We’ll make it out, right?” Anthony had asked, his voice fragile. Davar had nodded, gripping his partner's hand so hard he thought he might break it. But that was before. Before Anthony was gone, before the streets swallowed everything good.

He could hear the girl’s silent scream in his head, feel her terror. Someone had ripped the life from her, and they’d enjoyed it. Davar’s hand clenched around the butt of his cigarette until the ashes burned his fingertips. He didn’t feel the pain. He didn’t feel much of anything anymore—just the constant, dull weight of guilt that never let him breathe.

The air was heavy with the smell of copper, sweat, and sex. It was thick, suffocating. The rain wasn’t washing it away fast enough. It never did. Not in this part of town.

As Davar stood up, he saw the rookie cop hovering nearby, face pale, trying to act like he hadn’t already vomited in the shadows. “Jesus Christ, Commissioner,” the kid stammered. “What kind of monster does this?”

“Same kind that took everything from me,” Davar muttered under his breath. He didn’t look at the rookie. He couldn’t stand the pity in the kid’s eyes, the way they flickered with that faint hope he used to have. He stepped over the body, his shoes squelching in the mud and blood. The rain kept coming, but it wasn’t enough to cleanse what had been done here. Not even close.

The commissioner flicked his cigarette into the gutter and made his way back to his car. Inside, the silence weighed on him like a confession unspoken. His thoughts drifted to Anthony’s widow, Maria. He hadn’t seen her in years, but he couldn’t stop thinking about that last night in their apartment. Wine stains on the cushions, Anthony’s laugh filling the room, the taste of Maria’s lips on his, forbidden and desperate. She had wept against him, asking questions he didn’t have the courage to answer.
